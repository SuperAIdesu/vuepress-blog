One spring day last year, I took the train to the neighboring city to see Y's band performance. It was a show put up by the anime and video game music club of Y's university. Since joining the club months earlier, Y had been filling her life with rehearsals and hangouts with fellow bandmates. As a huge fan of band-themed fiction, I couldn't let go of the chance to witness a slice from the real life version of it.

After a sudden afternoon rainstorm passed and the night began to fall, groups of students (and me) filled most of the seats in the auditorium, and the bands started to play. Of course no one on stage was professional, but the performance was impressive nonetheless. The crowds were cheering, the performers were clearly enjoying themselves, and I was ingesting fresh youth enthusiasm.

During intermission, they left some background music playing. By the stage, I recognized R, Y's partner, who was a slightly androgynous white boy with long curly hair. As I watched him from afar, he sat down at the edge of the empty stage, picked up a microphone and began to sing to the music, karaoke style. It was a song from [LoveLive!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love_Live!), one of my favorite anime franchises. At that moment, he was singing casually and expressively, as if there weren't a roaming crowd in the room potentially watching. After one song ended, he just followed the music to the next. How free!

"I wanna sing like this someday." This was the thought that hit me next.

"Do you like Karaoke?" My SLP (the professional term for voice therapy doctor) asked during our first session.

"Not really", I answered.

"I was asking because another of my Chinese trans girl clients loves karaoke, so for her we had to do a lot more work on the singing front." She explained, "So that's good."

But my answer was grossly oversimplified.

I loved singing when I was a kid. As a result of my parents' insistence that I learn to play the piano, I had a great sense of pitch and could grasp sheet music quickly. My parents even sent me to singing lessons a few times, although I gave up soon due to my peculiar music taste (mostly English and Japanese, which bodes ill with Chinese musical classrooms).

When I started middle school, I got the freedom to hangout with friends without my parents present. And I quickly became fond of the newly installed karaoke boxes in the local shopping mall. They were basically soundproof phone booths with karaoke machines installed inside. Contrary to the more traditional KTV establishments, they were much cheaper, did not smell like tobacco, and did not require ID to use, thus perfect for teenagers like me. My friends and I frequented the mall on weekends and holidays, and we would usually pop in a karaoke box to sing a few songs after a long, bored stroll.

Days passed by and when I was slowly learning to sing anime songs using Romaji (Latin script for Japanese) lyrics, puberty sneaked up on me. One day I was singing in the karaoke box as usual, and one of my friends decided to record a video of me singing ["Masayume chasing"](https://youtu.be/aqRuzjIBkxY) (in hindsight, a very challenging song). The video was then sent into a larger friend group chat. Soon, laughs and ridicule filled my chat inbox.

"Am I really that bad?" With some defiance I opened the video. And that was the moment when I realized, painfully, that my voice has defied my best efforts to control, turning lower and more creaky. My body had changed in a terrifying way, and singing was never the same.

Deeply humiliated, I stopped going to karaoke for years. Until around when I graduated from high school, and had found a group of hardcore otaku (zealous anime or manga fan, sometimes derogatory) friends to fool around with.

Over the following summer and winter holidays, we would go to the only KTV in the city that offered an extensive Japanese song library, and stay there for multiple hours. They didn't care how good I can sing, as long as we can all have the time singing along to the latest and hottest J-POP and anime tracks. It was okay to shout, it was okay to rap to the lyrics, and it was okay to only sing the chorus part. We all had our mental complexes, and the dark rooms in the KTV seemed to have become a safe space to escape from the trappings of daily life.

But after the group dispersed to different cities or even countries, the space gradually disappeared, along with my familiarity with singing.

Once during my undergrad in China, I was "compelled" to sing. It was the mandatory school-wide   chorus competition, and freshmen from each department has to team up to perform in front the whole school. It was counted as part of the propaganda class credit requirement, so there was no escape.

On one of the cold autumn nights, after a full day of classes, all students from the math department that I belonged to convened in a large classroom to be assigned to voice types and learn about the assigned song for us. I didn't quite remember which voice type I was: Bass or Baritone? Probably Bass. There were a lot of Basses, and very few Sopranos and Altos, because in my cohort, only 10 percent of math students were women.

The conductor, who was an older student in the department, also had the talent to write the song for us. He insisted to put something "distinctly mathematical" in the song, so one line of the song's melody followed the digits of Pi (In Chinese [Jianpu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_musical_notation), 1 is the middle C, so 3.1415926 became $ECFCG\dot{D}DA$). As a result, it sounded rancid. Or maybe even well-written music wouldn't sound good on such an imbalanced chorus.
